\begin{abstract}
With the advancement of technology in three major fields, i.e., Artificial Intelligence, Computer Vision and Robotics over the last decade, autonomous systems have been increasingly advancing in performing complex real world tasks such as autonomous cleaning.
This work presents the navigation and trash detection subsystems of Swachh Bauddhika Yantra (SBY), an autonomous indoor cleaning robot with a robotic arm to pick up trash. The robot's hardware performs manual mode. The software was developed and tested in Gazebo simulator, while the trash detectors were trained on a custom-made dataset and benchmarked on Jetson Orin Nano. The navigation software builds a two dimensional map using the depth camera and wheel odometry sensors, selects the unexplored spaces as goals, follows planned paths with a predictive controller. The exploration covered a mean of 61.4\% of reachable floor over nine episodes, 86.3\% in a multi-room world being the highest. 29.2\% of the 178 navigation goals set on a known map were reached within 0.30 m of the true positions. For trash detection, a custom annotated dataset of 877 images consisting of 8 classes was created, and three detector models (YOLOv8n, YOLOv11n-seg and RF-DETR), all exceeding 0.92 mean average precision (mAP) at an overlap threshold of 0.5. On Jetson Orin Nano, the three detectors ran at a maximum of 79 frames per second (FPS) at half precision, drawing 12.7 to 14.9 watts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In recent years, as the technology has advanced, the convergence of artificial intelligence (AI), computer vision (CV), and robotics \cite{cohen2025fusion} has enabled the development of autonomous systems capable of performing complex real-world tasks such as indoor cleaning~\cite{ramalingam2022}. Existing approaches rely heavily on manual effort,
leading to inconsistency and lower productivity, especially in large indoor spaces~\cite{memmesheimer2024cleaning}. Because of this limitation, development of automated intelligent cleaning systems can help reduce human intervention.
Such systems can also improve efficiency and performance~\cite{jagtapexploring}. These systems will consist of various component like Path Planning, Trash detection, Picking of Trash, Vacuum cleaning of dust and Floor cleaning~\cite{megalingam2025cleaning}. This work focuses on developing Path Planning, Trash detection and Vacuum cleaning. The feasibility of the work is supported by the use of existing technologies such as NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano for real-time processing~\cite{nvidia2023jetson}, Visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) for mapping~\cite{campos2021orb}, and deep learning models for object detection such as You Only Look Once (YOLO) version 8 (YOLOv8) ~\cite{varghese2024yolov8}. Prior research and existing implementations including autonomous cleaning robots~\cite{prayash2019designing}, Internet of Things (IoT) enabled systems~\cite{burman2020iot}, and SLAM based navigation~\cite{al2024review} indicate that these
 components are individually well understood and implementable \cite{loganathan2023systematic}. Leveraging these technologies, this work is based upon it.

Current systems, both industrial and commercial, largely focus on floor cleaning tasks such as vacuuming, with advanced navigation and obstacle avoidance capabilities~\cite{eltwab2025intelligent}. Existing studies address navigation, IoT integration~\cite{hussin2024smart}, and cleaning efficiency, but do not provide an end-to-end solution combining object detection, robot motion control, and obstacle avoidance capabilities~\cite{memmesheimer2024cleaning}. This gap shows the need for more exploration and supports the choice of this work as important.

The novelty of this work lies in setting foundation of combining multiple subsystems of autonomous navigation, AI-based trash detection, and robotic arm-based waste collection into a centralized platform. Unlike conventional cleaning robots that clean surfaces \cite{eltwab2025intelligent}, this system actively identifies, classifies, and collects the waste objects. To support different operational needs, the system offers manual, semi-automatic, and fully automatic modes allowing flexibility based on use-case requirements. The inclusion of omnidirectional mobility further enhances motion in constrained indoor environments~\cite{patruno2024vision}. This overall integration represents a significant advancement over existing individual subsystems~\cite{nahavandi2025comprehensive}.

This paper reports the navigation and trash-detection subsystems of Swachh Bauddhika
Yantra (SBY), an indoor cleaning robot with robotic arm for picking up trash, with it hardware prototype performing manual mode.

% Swachh Bauddhika Yantra addresses the need for autonomous indoor cleaning systems that can navigate, detect waste, and take appropriate cleaning actions. The proposed system combines perception, mapping, decision-making, and actuation into a single robot platform.
The main contributions of this work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item  To develop navigation system for the prototype of SBY and testing in the gazebo simulated environment by utilizing appropriate SLAM techniques.

    \item To implement deep learning based Trash detection modules for real time identification and classification of class of trash objects for robotic arm of SBY.

\item To create custom dataset for trash detection to train deep learning models.

\item To assess the performance of the subsystems of SBY by analyzing the evaluation metrics such as mapping accuracy, trash detection metrics (mean average precision,mAP), overall detection accuracy, floor coverage and navigation accuracy.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Trash Detection and Classification}
The authors of ~\cite{mustapha2025hybrid} proposed a hybrid deep learning model combining YOLOv8 for object localisation with a convolutional neural network (CNN) classifier for compost versus non-compost waste categorisation, achieving an accuracy of 88.3\%. The proposed work ~\cite{xiao2024enhancing} enhanced YOLOv8 for waste detection by introducing an Adaptive Inner-IOU bounding box regression mechanism and a FlexiC2f feature fusion module, improving mAP over the standard YOLOv8 baseline. The researchers of ~\cite{arishi2025real} trained a YOLOv8-CBAM model incorporating five data augmentation techniques and two attention mechanisms for real-time household waste detection, achieving a mean mAP of 89.5\% to a 4.2\% improvement over the standard YOLOv8 baseline. A comparative evaluation of YOLO versions v8 through v12 for smart real-time waste sorting was conducted in ~\cite{azizi2025real}, where YOLOv12 achieved a mean average precision of 78\%, outperforming earlier YOLO variants across the evaluated waste categories. The authors of ~\cite{vieri2024enhancing} trained multiple YOLOv8 variants on a custom 7,130-image waste dataset, with YOLOv8l recording an mAP@0.5:0.95 of 89.7\%, an mAP@0.5 of 93\%.

The proposed work ~\cite{sumon2026floating} benchmarked YOLOv8 (v8-v10), RT-DETR, and Faster R-CNN variants for floating waste detection on inland waterways, where RT-DETR-X recorded the highest individual mAP50 of 86.3\%,a weighted box fusion ensemble of Faster R-CNN and RT-DETR-L further improved the mAP50 to 88.3\%. The researchers of ~\cite{wu2024research} optimised YOLOv8 for intelligent solid waste classification using attention modules and an enhanced feature pyramid network, recording improvements of 5.4\% in mAP@0.5 and 6.1\% in mAP@0.5:0.95 over the standard YOLOv8s baseline on a self-constructed solid waste dataset. The authors of ~\cite{chen2022robot} developed an autonomous construction and demolition waste sorting robot integrating a custom SLAM method for real-time localisation demonstrating robust identification of multiple C\&D waste categories across complex site environments. The proposed work ~\cite{lahoti2024multi} presents a multi-class waste segregation system employing YOLOv5 for detection interfaced with a 3D-printed robotic arm yielding an 80\% segregation accuracy. The researchers of ~\cite{romano2025deep} proposed a deep learning method for floating garbage debris recognition on a high-performance edge computing platform, achieving real-time throughput suitable for unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)-mounted and waterway monitoring deployments.

A vision based approach for waste object localisation and geometric feature extraction from point clouds was presented in~\cite{aarthi2023vision}, enabling a robotic manipulator to accurately estimate grasp poses on irregularly shaped refuse items for autonomous floor-level pickup. The authors of ~\cite{lv2023machine} implemented machine learning-based garbage detection combined with depth-based 3D spatial localization to derive precise grasp coordinates, allowing an intelligent robotic system to autonomously pick up waste items placed in unstructured floor environments.

Analogous to the human sensory system, the visual inputs are obtained from the environment via Logitech C270 HD webcam\cite{logitechC270} and Intel\textregistered{} RealSense\texttrademark{} Depth Camera D435i \cite{intel_d435i} both streaming at 30 fps simultaneously. Additionally wheel encoders are used to receive accurate rotational feedback to calculate distance travelled. 

On receiving the inputs from Interface and Sensor Layer, Processing Layer consists of two major modules, i.e., Object Detection and Visual SLAM. Trash objects and Obstacles are classified by the Object Detection Module using trained Deep-Learning models. The Navigation functionality is handled by the Visual SLAM module, further comprising of Mapping and Localization sub modules. Visual Odometry sub-module is responsible for visual feature extraction and matching to estimate the robot's current position. This data is utilised by the mapping and localization submodules to create and update the occupancy map along with the robot's estimated pose. This information is further utilized by the Path Planning, Navigation and obstacle avoidance sub-modules to derive feasible and safe paths along with dynamic decision making ensuring reliable navigation. Consolidating the output of the preceding sub-modules, the Decision Module, which is considered as the ``orchestrator'' of the system, fuses the Perception and Navigation information to compute a simple high level action which is delivered to the Actuator Layer.

The Actuator Layer is the final layer of the Architecture marking the transition between Intelligence and real world Action. Arduino Uno microcontroller in this layer translates the high level actions provided by the decision module into low level signals which is passed on to the appropriate actuator which physically take action on the environment such as vacuuming via vacuum cleaner, picking up trash via robotic arm. The Actuator Layer is actuator agnostic, designed for extending the robot's functionality by connecting new actuators to Arduino Uno.

The robot has three levels made using a metal chassis. It is rectangular in shape, with a length of 60 cm and width of 50 cm. These levels have metal supports. Two ~15.2cm (6-inch) brushless DC (BLDC) hub motors are mounted at the rear, one on each side. A free wheel is placed at the front centre and carries around 10 kg. The hub motors are controlled using YL 12, 24 V sine-wave controllers, it is mounted below the chassis. 
Bottom rack: This section accommodates two lead-acid batteries with a weight of 10 kg each. The robotic arm is fixed on the robot’s front end, just above the free wheel.

\subsection{Hardware and Communication Setup}
At start-up the Jetson self-checks the cameras, Arduino and wheel drivers. The Arduino isolates the Jetson from the actuators and executes simple, actuator-agnostic commands such as moving forward 30 cm, turning right 15$^{\circ}$, or switching on the vacuum. NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano can be considered as the ``brain'' of the system. Decisions and commands originate from here and trickles down all the way till actuators. It handles the computation locally including perception, mapping and decision making process. A separate Arduino Uno microcontroller is designated for handling low level actuator control. The reason for this separation is that it detaches high level computational process from low level actions by translating the decisions into executable actions performed by actuators.

Perception begins with two cameras and the depth sensor along with feedback from the Wheel-mounted encoders. Jetson consolidates the visual and rotational inputs, performs computation and sends the final decision to Arduino Uno.

Prior to the end to end decision making, a communication channel is established between each of the components i.e., between sensors, Jetson, Arduino and the actuators. the visual sensors are connected to Jetson via USB-A cable streaming at 1280x720 pixels \& 30fps, while Jetson and Arduino agree on a serial link at a baud rate of 115200 bidirectionally and a consistent 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit (8N1) frame format for data transfer. Communication happens via a custom newline terminated ASCII string commands (e.g., MOVE:FORWARD).For each command, there is an equivalent feedback via acknowledgment message from Arduino along with status updates periodically at a rate of 1Hz. This protocol was adopted in order to simplify debugging, while maintaining minimal overhead on the microcontroller.

Arduino Uno parses the ASCII commands received to interpret them using a set of well defined mapping between commands and the low level actions which are then passed onto the actuators.

Each actuator connects to Arduino over a specific set of pins. Arduino upon receiving the ASCII command (e.g., SPEED:8; MOVE:FORWARD), translates into equivalent pulse-width modulation (PWM) signal along with motor direction control via relay switching. Actuators collect the low level actions from the Arduino through the relay pins and performs the designated actions.

The power supply for all the hardware components is provided by a 24V, 26Ah rechargeable battery, which provides roughly 2 hours of operation time rechargeable via a standard 230V, 50Hz AC mains supply.
Each of the components require varying input power supply which is handled by usage of DC-to-DC buck converter responsible performing voltage step down action and provide a stable constant voltage supply tailored to specific component.

\subsection{Operating Modes}
The three operating modes are defined for the SBY. Manual mode has been implemented completely.
\subsubsection*{Manual Mode} 
The manual mode involves the operator to have the complete flexibility in controlling every aspect of cleaning process. The operator monitors the cleaning area continuosly through a human machine interface (HMI), this allows them to make informed decision regarding robot's movement like speed, direction of movement, cleaning area, and tasks like vacuuming, spot cleaning. Additionally, the HMI also shows warnings,alerts \& robot status which helps the operator to identify any issues or malfunctions during cleaning tasks.This mode is ideal for situations where precise control is required, such as cleaning in high-traffic or sensitive areas, or when specific conditions need to be met during cleaning.

\subsubsection*{Semi-Automatic Mode} 
Semi-Automatic mode reduces the manual need for control by introducing few autonomous features.This mode maintains some level of control while robot is performing  much of the cleaning process autonomously. The operator's role involves selecting the specific area to be cleaned, choosing the type of cleaning (e.g.,mopping,vacuuming,scrubbing), and defining the duration for the task. Once these parameters are set, the robot takes over and
executes the cleaning operation within the defined area. This mode reduces the need for constant
manual intervention, allowing the operator to manage multiple tasks simultaneously. Similar to
Manual Mode, the operator can view a continuous video feed, monitor real-time analysis, and
receive system alerts. However, with less hands-on control required, this mode provides a more
efficient balance of human oversight and robotic autonomy, making it ideal for routine or less
complex cleaning tasks.

\subsubsection*{Automatic Mode} 
An autonomous mode functions independently without the input from the operator. In this mode, the robot uses its mapping technology to autonomously detect the areas that require cleaning, determine the best course of action, and perform the necessary cleaning tasks. The robot’s object detection algorithms decide whether vacuuming, mopping, or another cleaning operation is needed, adjusting its approach as the environment changes. Throughout the process, the operator is still able to view the video feed and ongoing analysis, and any relevant system alerts will be displayed. However, the robot handles the entire cleaning procedure autonomously, making this mode particularly useful for large or complex areas that require continuous, hands-off operation. With minimal operator intervention, the robot can effectively and efficiently maintain cleanliness without requiring constant supervision.

\subsection{Dataset Description}
Although existing datasets such as TrashNet and ImageNet were available, they contained classes that did not match the specific class labels required for the work. There were uncertainties, such as mismatches in the environment and surroundings, which limited their applicability. Another problem encountered was that the images in the existing datasets were captured from the human eye level. Furthermore, these public datasets were created using high-quality cameras, which did not align with the experimental setup that uses a Red Green Blue (RGB) camera.

The initial dataset was created in a laboratory environment under controlled conditions. The trash objects were captured against diverse backgrounds, in different orientations, at varying speeds, and under different lighting conditions to reflect real-world environments and minimize potential bias. Sample images of each class are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset_samples}. An RGB camera with a resolution of $1280 \times 720$~pixels was mounted on the robot at a low height with a downward inclination of 30 degrees to capture the trash objects. When the camera was mounted on top of the robot, the trash objects were not clearly visible for accurate detection. Therefore, the decision to use a 30-degree inclined camera angle was intentional to increase the area of visibility, enable more accurate detection of trash objects, and ensure that the robotic arm could reach the trash object for pickup.

The process took 10 man-hours, with the efforts of two people, to collect the trash and create the initial dataset of 877 images. A key design choice in this work was to define object classes according to the cleaning action they require rather than purely based on the object identity. This approach allows the output of the object detection model to be used directly by the Decision Fusion Module (DFM) (Table~\ref{tab:decision_logic}). The six classes and their image counts are presented in Table~\ref{tab:raw_dataset_distribution}.

\subsection{Instance Segmentation}
During the annotation process, an instance segmentation approach was adopted instead of directly drawing bounding boxes. Unlike conventional object detection, which represents each object using an approximate rectangular bounding box, instance segmentation identifies the precise pixel-level boundaries of every object instance. This enables the annotation to closely follow the actual shape and extent of irregularly shaped objects such as aluminium foil, paper, wrappers, and dust, which cannot be accurately represented using simple rectangular boxes.


After image acquisition, all images were annotated using the Roboflow annotation platform \cite{roboflow}. The Smart Select tool was used to assist in accurately outlining object boundaries during the annotation process. However, no auto-labeling or pre-trained annotation models were employed, and every object instance was manually annotated by two annotators. Bounding boxes were assigned to each annotated object, including the six waste categories as well as the floor and obstacle classes. All annotations were manually verified to ensure consistency in class labels and annotation quality. The annotated dataset was exported in the YOLO format for training the YOLO models and in the  Common Objects in Context (COCO) format for training the RF-DETR model. 

The final annotated dataset comprises 877 images containing a total of 6,349 bounding box annotations across the eight object classes that map onto the six functional classes described in Table~\ref{tab:raw_dataset_distribution}. The per-category annotation counts are as follows: obstacle - 3,517, floor - 917, paper - 691, bottle - 351, wrapper - 283, aluminium\_foil - 259, cement\_dust - 169, and wooden\_dust - 162. A class imbalance exists between obstacle class and the dust class with obstacle annotations exceeding wooden\_dust by a factor of approximately 21.7. This imbalance reflects the real-world distribution of objects seen during robot navigation and was addressed through augmentation of under represented classes during training. The dataset was split into training, validation and test following a 70:20:10 ratio resulting in 611 training images, 175 validation images and 91 test images. Augmentation was performed only after the dataset split, ensuring a clear separation between training and evaluation data and preventing overlap between original and augmented samples.


\subsection{Augmentation}
Augmentation was applied to the training split only, using Albumentations with horizontal and vertical flips, rotation (±15°), random cropping, perspective warping, brightness and contrast rotation ($\pm 15^{\circ}$) / changes ($\pm 30\%$) and 5×5 motion blur. Each operation was applied with probability 0.5 and could be combined, to mimic camera orientation, lighting and motion blur. Validation and test sets were not augmented.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}
The internal weights of a deep learning model are continuously learned and updated via backpropagation during training, whereas hyperparameters are the choices that must be explicitly defined before a single image is processed. These external variables, such as the learning rate, weight decay, batch size, and input resolution (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}), influence how the model learns, how quickly it converges, and its overall performance. The relationship between hyperparameters and model accuracy is very complex. Since every dataset is different and the training process is unpredictable, there is no single formula that can determine the best hyperparameter values beforehand. Instead, they are usually found through experimentation or optimization methods such as Grid Search, Random Search, Bayesian Optimization, or other algorithms. Different combinations must be tested to identify the configuration that yields the highest model accuracy.



\begin{table}[!t]
\centering
\caption{Hyperparameters considered during model optimization and their roles.}\footnotesize\setlength{\extrarowheight}{3pt}

\begin{tabular}{|m{0.24\columnwidth}|m{0.66\columnwidth}|}
\hline
\textbf{Hyperparameter} & \textbf{What it Controls} \\ \hline
Learning Rate ($lr$) & Size of each gradient-descent step during optimization. \\ \hline
Batch Size & Number of images processed before each gradient update. \\ \hline
Input Resolution & Pixel dimensions of the input images fed to the model. \\ \hline
Gradient Accumulation & Number of micro-batches processed before performing a weight update (effective batch size multiplier). \\ \hline
Weight Decay & L2 regularization strength that penalizes large weights to reduce overfitting. \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:hyperparameters}
\end{table}

Grid Search is a hyperparameter optimization technique that evaluates all possible combinations of the given parameter values. However, it is a poor choice for this specific pipeline. Random Search is a hyperparameter optimization technique that samples values from predefined distributions instead of testing all possible combinations. Bayesian Optimization takes a smarter approach by treating hyperparameter tuning as a mathematical optimization problem and learning from past results to decide which configuration to evaluate next.

The reason why Random Search was implemented is that the search space has five dimensions: \textit{learning rate} ($lr$) and \textit{weight decay} are continuous hyperparameters, \textit{batch size} and \textit{input resolution} each have three possible options, and \textit{gradient accumulation} has two possible options. A full Grid Search would therefore require $5\ (\textit{lr}) \times 3\ (\textit{batch}) \times 3\ (\textit{resolution}) \times 2\ (\textit{gradient accumulation}) \times 5\ (\textit{weight decay}) = 450$ trials. Considering that each trial takes approximately 20 minutes, Grid Search would require over six days of continuous GPU time solely for hyperparameter optimization. Furthermore, Grid Search spends a significant amount of time exploring less important dimensions of the search space, whereas Random Search evaluates a unique combination in every trial, providing a much richer exploration of the most influential hyperparameters.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

Object detection is the foundational computer vision task that enables the robotic vacuum cleaner to perceive, classify, and act upon objects in its environment. Unlike simple image classification, which assigns a single label to an entire image, object detection simultaneously localises every object (via a bounding box) and assigns it a semantic class. Two families of detectors dominate modern real-time deployments: the RF-DETR and the You Only Look Once (YOLO) family \cite{yolo_review}. Both belong to the single-stage detection paradigm, they predict class probabilities and bounding-box offsets in a single forward pass, without a separate region-proposal stage. This property is critical for robotic applications where inference latency directly constrains navigation speed, and prior benchmarking on embedded hardware confirms that both families are capable of real-time throughput on modern edge accelerators.

\subsubsection{You Only Look Once (YOLO)}

\paragraph{Architecture Overview.}
YOLO \cite{yolo_review} frames object detection as a single regression problem from raw pixels directly to bounding-box coordinates and class probabilities. Over successive versions, the YOLO family has evolved substantially in backbone design, loss formulation, and training strategy, while maintaining its signature single-pass inference speed \cite{yolo_review}.

In this robotic system, YOLOv8s (the small variant of Ultralytics YOLOv8) is deployed for per-frame detection of debris, obstacles, hazards, and humans, while a companion YOLOv8-seg model handles segmentation-based floor-state inference every fifth frame. YOLOv8's architecture has three principal components. The \emph{backbone} is a Cross Stage Partial (CSP)-Darknet enriched with C2f (Cross-Stage Feature Fusion) modules, which improve gradient flow by splitting feature maps into parallel branches. The \emph{neck} is a Path Aggregation Network and Feature Pyramid Network (PAN+FPN) + Feature Pyramid Network that fuses features across three scales (P3, P4, P5). The \emph{head} is a decoupled detection head that separates classification and regression into two parallel branches, accelerating convergence and improving accuracy compared to earlier coupled-head designs.

A key distinction in YOLOv8 is its \emph{anchor-free} detection strategy: the model directly regresses the distances from a predicted box centre to all four box edges, simplifying the training pipeline and removing the need for anchor hyperparameter tuning entirely.

\paragraph{Mathematical Description.}
Let the input image be resized to $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$ (typically $640 \times 640$). The network produces predictions at three stride levels $s \in \{8, 16, 32\}$, yielding feature grids of size $\frac{H}{s} \times \frac{W}{s}$. At each grid location $(i, j)$, the model predicts:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{ij} = \left[\underbrace{t_x,\, t_y,\, t_w,\, t_h}_{\text{box regression}},\; \underbrace{o}_{\text{objectness}},\; \underbrace{p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_C}_{\text{class scores}}\right]
\end{equation}
Box edges are predicted via Distribution Focal Loss (DFL), which models each edge location as a discrete probability distribution over integer values $\{0, 1, \ldots, R\}$:
\begin{equation}
\hat{d} = \sum_{k=0}^{R} k \cdot \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{R+1}$ is the raw regression output and $\hat{d}$ is the predicted distance from the grid-cell centre to one box edge. The final box coordinates are:
\begin{equation}
x_1 = (c_x - \hat{d}_\text{left}) \cdot s, \quad x_2 = (c_x + \hat{d}_\text{right}) \cdot s
\end{equation}
and analogously for $y_1, y_2$. The overall YOLOv8s architecture used in this work is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:yolo_arch}.

  \centering  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.45\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/yolov8s archi 2.0.png}
  \caption{YOLOv8 network architecture}
  \label{fig:yolo_arch}
\end{figure*}

\subsubsection{RF-DETR}
\paragraph{Architecture Overview:}
Roboflow DEtection TRansformer (RF-DETR)~\cite{robinson2026rfdetr} is a real-time detection transformer developed by Roboflow that reformulates object detection as a direct set-prediction problem, removing hand-designed components such as anchor generation and non-maximum suppression (NMS) that YOLO-family detectors depend on. Building on the original DEtection TRansformer (DETR) formulation \cite{carion2020detr} and incorporating deformable attention for faster convergence \cite{zhu2020deformable}, RF-DETR combines a pretrained DINOv2 \cite{oquab2023dinov2} vision transformer backbone with a lightweight multi-scale deformable decoder, allowing it to inherit strong general-purpose visual features while remaining fast enough for real-time inference. The model family is produced via weight-sharing neural architecture search (NAS) over the backbone-decoder configuration space, giving a range of accuracy-latency operating points from Nano to 2X-Large \cite{robinson2026rfdetr}. Unlike YOLOv8's convolutional backbone-neck-head pipeline, RF-DETR's self-attention layers model long-range spatial dependencies directly across the image, which is useful for distinguishing visually similar debris classes (e.g., wooden dust versus cement dust) using global context rather than local texture alone. On the COCO benchmark, RF-DETR was reported as the first real-time model (25+ FPS on an NVIDIA T4) to exceed 60 mAP, and it is Pareto-optimal relative to YOLO-family models at comparable latencies \cite{robinson2026rfdetr}.

The model predicts a fixed number of $N$ object queries per image, each producing a class label and bounding box. A bipartite matching step during training assigns each ground-truth object to exactly one predicted query using the Hungarian algorithm, removing the need for anchor boxes, Intersection over Union (IoU) thresholds, or NMS post-processing at inference time \cite{carion2020detr}. Because RF-DETR retains this end-to-end formulation while adopting a NAS-optimised, latency-aware backbone configuration, it targets the same real-time operating point as YOLOv8s and YOLOv11n, making it suitable for comparison in this work despite of its different architecture.

\paragraph{Mathematical Description.}
Given an input image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$, the DINOv2 backbone produces a spatial feature map which is projected across multiple scales and passed through the deformable-attention decoder together with $N$ learned object queries $\{q_1, \ldots, q_N\}$. Each query is decoded into a prediction
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i = \left(\hat{c}_i,\ \hat{b}_i\right), \quad \hat{c}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{C+1},\ \hat{b}_i \in [0,1]^4
\end{equation}
where $\hat{c}_i$ is the class probability distribution (including a ``no object'' class) and $\hat{b}_i$ is the normalised box in $(c_x, c_y, w, h)$ form.

Given the set of ground-truth objects $\{y_j\}_{j=1}^{M}$ (padded with ``no object'' to size $N$), training finds the optimal bipartite assignment $\hat{\sigma}$ that minimises a matching cost via the Hungarian algorithm \cite{carion2020detr}:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\sigma} = \arg\min_{\sigma \in \mathfrak{S}_N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}_{\text{match}}\left(y_i,\ \hat{y}_{\sigma(i)}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{match}}$ combines a classification cost with a box cost. Once matched, the model is optimised end-to-end with a loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{cls}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} + \lambda_{\text{L1}} \|b_i - \hat{b}_{\hat{\sigma}(i)}\|_1 + \lambda_{\text{giou}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{giou}}\left(b_i, \hat{b}_{\hat{\sigma}(i)}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{giou}}$ is the generalised IoU loss, penalising box misalignment even when predicted and ground-truth boxes do not overlap \cite{carion2020detr}. RF-DETR additionally applies auxiliary losses on intermediate decoder layers and a Group DETR-style parallel-decoder training scheme to accelerate convergence \cite{robinson2026rfdetr}. Because each query is matched to at most one object, duplicate detections are suppressed implicitly by the training objective rather than by a separate NMS step at inference. The overall RF-DETR architecture used in this work is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rfdetr_arch}.

\section{Navigation and Path Planning}
\label{sec:navigation}
The navigation system combines mapping, exploration and path planning to navigate unknown environments. RTAB-Map provides the map and robot pose, the frontier explorer selects the next goal, and Nav2 plans and executes the path while avoiding obstacles.

\subsection{Visual SLAM for Mapping and Localisation}
\label{sec:vslam}

The choice of RTAB-Map is justified in Section~\ref{sec:rtabmap}. This section describes how it is configured (Table~\ref{tab:rtabmap_params}) and what it provides to the rest of the navigation stack. RTAB-Map takes the colour and depth images of the D435i together with wheel odometry, and outputs a 2D occupancy grid and the \texttt{map}-to-\texttt{odom} transform. The Nav2 costmaps use the grid as their static layer, and the frontier explorer reads it to find unexplored space.

% \paragraph{Inputs.}
RTAB-Map operates in RGB-D mode with wheel odometry, which converts the wheel angular velocities into the base velocities as
\begin{equation}
v = \frac{r\,(\omega_L + \omega_R)}{2}, \qquad \omega = \frac{r\,(\omega_R - \omega_L)}{L},
\end{equation}
where $v$ and $\omega$ are the linear and angular velocities of the robot base, $\omega_L$ and $\omega_R$ are the angular velocities of the left and right wheels, $r$ is the wheel radius, and $L$ is the distance between the wheels. The experiments reported in this paper use the wheel parameters of the simulated robot.

% \paragraph{Role of vision.}
Images are used for loop-closure detection and proximity-based node linking rather than frame-to-frame motion estimation. Since the robot operates on a flat floor, pose registration was restricted to three DOF

% \paragraph{Graph construction and loop closure.}
% A new pose-graph node is created whenever the robot moves 0.10~m or rotates 0.087~rad (approximately $5^{\circ}$), while the map is updated at 1~Hz. For each image, up to 500 GFTT keypoints are detected and described using BRIEF, with features restricted to a depth range of 0.1 - 4.0~m. The resulting descriptors are used for appearance-based loop-closure detection through a bag-of-words model. A loop closure is accepted when its likelihood exceeds 0.11 and geometric verification identifies at least 10 inliers. Spatial proximity detection is also enabled to establish links between nearby nodes,including those with different visual appearances.

Node creation, map update rate, keypoint limits, and loop-closure thresholds are listed in Table~\ref{tab:rtabmap_params}. The descriptors feed a bag of words model which is then used for an appearance based loop closure detection.

% \paragraph{Memory.}

Since no time or memory limits are configured, nodes are not transferred to  long-term memory during the experiments. Consequently, RTAB-Map's long-term memory management is not evaluated in this experiment.

\subsection{Path Planning}
\label{sec:planning}
Path planning in Swachh Bauddhika Yantra is handled at two levels. The frontier-based explorer selects the next exploration goal,while Nav2 computes and executes a path to that goal. Both rely on the occupancy grid and pose estimate provided by RTAB-Map.

% \paragraph{Goal selection.}
The robot uses a custom frontier-based explorer to select its next exploration goal (Algorithm~\ref{alg:frontier}) . The explorer processes the latest occupancy grid at 2~Hz and identifies frontier cells as free cells with at least one unknown 4-connected neighbour. Adjacent frontier cells are grouped into clusters using flood fill within a 0.5~m window. Clusters containing fewer than five cells are discarded, along with frontiers shorter than 0.5~m or those associated with recent navigation failures. The remaining candidates are ranked using the following utility function:
\begin{equation}
S = 1.0\,\ln(1 + n) \;-\; 1.5\,d \;-\; 0.8\,p,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of cells in the frontier, used as a proxy for the information a visit would reveal, and $d$ is the straight-line distance in metres from the robot to the frontier centroid. The last term, $p = \max(0,\,1 - d_{\mathrm{obs}}/0.5)$, penalises centroids near obstacles, where $d_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is the distance to the nearest occupied cell within 0.5~m of the centroid ($p = 0$ if there is none).

Since frontier centroids lie in unknown space, they cannot be used directly as navigation goals. Each centroid is instead mapped to the nearest known free cell, with limited search radius (Table~\ref{tab:planning_params}). Frontiers are blacklisted if no valid cell is found or if the resulting goal is within 0.30~m of the robot. The remaining goals are sent to Nav2.

While a goal is active, the explorer monitors navigation progress and cancels the goal if Nav2 reports a failure, the execution time exceeds 30~s, or the robot moves less than 0.30~m within 15~s. In-place rotation is also counted as motion. The corresponding frontier is then blacklisted for 60~s. Following successful navigation, a frontier is also blacklisted if it remains within 0.5~m of the reached goal and fewer than 10 new free cells have been discovered. This prevents repeated visits to regions that yield little new information.

If no map is available within 3~s of start-up, the robot rotates at 0.4~rad/s for 8~s to build an initial map. Exploration terminates when five consecutive detection cycles find no frontier, the number of free cells increases by fewer than 10 over 15~s, and the robot is stationary, with a linear speed below 0.02~m/s. Algorithm~\ref{alg:frontier} summarises the exploration loop.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Frontier exploration loop.}
\label{alg:frontier}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require occupancy grid $M$ (updated by RTAB-Map); blacklist $B \leftarrow \emptyset$ of frontier centroids, each expiring 60~s after its last failure; empty-cycle counter $c \leftarrow 0$
\If{no map exists 3~s after start}
    \State rotate at 0.4~rad/s for 8~s
\EndIf
\While{exploration is not complete}
    \State $F \leftarrow$ frontier clusters of $M$ at 2~Hz (flood fill, 0.5~m window, at least 5 cells)
    \State $F \leftarrow \{f \in F : \mathrm{length}(f) \geq 0.5~\mathrm{m}$ and the centroid of $f$ is more than 0.5~m from every entry of $B\}$
    \If{$F = \emptyset$}
        \State $c \leftarrow c + 1$
        \If{$c \geq 5$, free cells grew by fewer than 10 over 15~s, and linear speed is below 0.02~m/s}
            \State stop: exploration complete
        \EndIf
    \Else
        \State $c \leftarrow 0$
        \State $f^{*} \leftarrow \arg\max_{f \in F} S(f)$
        \State $g \leftarrow$ nearest free cell to the centroid of $f^{*}$ with clearance $\geq 0.30$~m, within 1.5~m
        \If{$g$ does not exist or lies within 0.30~m of the robot}
            \State add the centroid of $f^{*}$ to $B$
        \Else
            \State send $g$ to Nav2 and monitor it
            \If{Nav2 fails, 30~s elapse, or for 15~s the robot moves less than 0.30~m and does not rotate}
                \State cancel the goal and add the centroid of $f^{*}$ to $B$
            \ElsIf{the goal is reached but a frontier within 0.5~m of it persists with fewer than 10 new free cells}
                \State add that frontier's centroid to $B$
            \EndIf
        \EndIf
    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% \paragraph{Global planning.}
Nav2 uses the Smac Hybrid-A* planner to compute a path to each selected goal. The planner searches over position and heading using the Dubins motion model, producing forward-only paths subject to a minimum turning radius of 0.25~m. The goal tolerance is set to 0.30~m, unknown space is treated as traversable to support exploration, and the planning time is limited to 5~s.

The global costmap uses the SLAM map at a resolution of 5~cm. It combines the RTAB-Map occupancy grid with a spatio-temporal voxel layer fed by the depth camera and an inflation layer with a radius of 0.28~m and a cost scaling factor of 5. Collision checking uses the robot's rectangular footprint of $0.30 \times 0.25$~m.

% \paragraph{Local control.}
The planned path is followed by a Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) controller running at 20~Hz on a $4 \times 4$~m rolling local costmap, which is updated at 5~Hz and uses the same three layers. In each cycle the controller samples 2000 velocity sequences over a 2.8~s horizon (56 steps of 0.05~s) with a differential-drive model, and ranks them with eight cost terms: constraint, footprint collision cost, goal, goal angle, path alignment, path following, path angle and a preference for forward motion. Forward speed is limited to 0.30~m/s and angular speed to 1.0~rad/s, and a velocity smoother bounds the acceleration at 2.5~m/s$^2$ and 3.2~rad/s$^2$. A goal counts as reached within 0.30~m and 0.5~rad. Table~\ref{tab:planning_params} lists the settings and marks those changed from the Nav2 defaults.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

All experiments used the Swachh Bauddhika Yantra dataset, a custom trash object dataset with the eight classes: aluminium foil, bottle, cement dust, floor, obstacle, paper, wooden dust, and wrapper. Training images were augmented as described in Section~\ref{sec:trash_detection}; the validation split (175 images) was left unaugmented.
Three architectures were compared: YOLOv8n, YOLOv11n-seg, and RF-DETR. YOLOv8n is a nano-scale single-stage detector trained for 100 epochs. YOLOv11n-seg is a nano-scale instance segmentation model trained for 60 epochs with early stopping, and its bounding-box and mask heads were evaluated separately. RF-DETR is a transformer-based detector trained for 99 epochs, and both its regular and exponential moving average (EMA) checkpoints were assessed. All models were trained on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (48~GB) with mixed-precision training, and hyperparameters were selected through a random search of five trials of 20 epochs each. YOLOv11n-seg used the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of $1.22\times10^{-4}$, a batch size of 8, and $560\times560$ input. RF-DETR used AdamW with an EMA decay of 0.993, a batch size of 16, and $448\times448$ input.

Performance was measured using COCO-standard metrics: mean Average Precision at IoU thresholds of 0.5 (mAP@0.5) and 0.75 (mAP@0.75), averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 (mAP@0.5:0.95), along with precision and recall. For the YOLO models, mAP@0.75 was obtained from a dedicated validation run. For RF-DETR, per-class average precision (AP@0.5) was additionally computed at the final epoch to examine class-level behavior.

\subsection{Trash Detection Performance}
\label{subsec:performance}

All three models achieved strong detection performance on the validation set, with mAP@0.5 above 0.92 in every case (Table~\ref{tab:overall}). RF-DETR obtained the highest mAP@0.5 of 0.9336, followed closely by YOLOv11n-seg (box head) at 0.9302 and YOLOv8n at 0.9271. The difference between the best and worst model is less than 0.7\% indicating that at a safe IoU threshold the three architectures are nearly equivalent. The differences become clearer under stricter localization criteria. RF-DETR achieved the highest mAP@0.5:0.95, at 0.8387 for the regular checkpoint and 0.8432 for the EMA checkpoint, compared with 0.8208 for YOLOv11n-seg and 0.8195 for YOLOv8n. This margin of roughly 1.8\% to 2.4\% suggests that RF-DETR produces tighter bounding boxes across a range of IoU thresholds. At mAP@0.75, YOLOv11n-seg led with 0.8895 ahead of YOLOv8n 0.8817 and RF-DETR 0.8795, so RF-DETR's advantage comes from its mean performance across thresholds and not from any single strict threshold.

Dust has diffuse texture and unclear boundaries, which bounding boxes represent poorly, and the obstacle class is highly varied (RF-DETR 77.3\%). Segmentation masks or more dust and obstacle data are the natural next steps.

The absolute trajectory error of the SLAM pose against ground truth, without scale correction, was 0.57~m on average (interval 0.33 to 0.86~m), and the relative error was 0.25~m per metre travelled. Contact with obstacles was the main source of large errors. In 4 of the 9 episodes, wheel slip during contact corrupted the heading given by wheel odometry, and RTAB-Map did not recover from it. Excluding these episodes, the trajectory error was 0.44~m (3 episodes), and before the first corruption it was 0.33~m (7 episodes). Because the corrupted episodes also affect the coverage and completeness figures above, those figures should be read as an estimate of what the full pipeline achieves in simulation, not of the best case. This work does not quantify the improvement provided by loop closures over raw odometry, as the trajectory error of wheel odometry alone was not measured.

% \paragraph{Loop closure.}
A loop closure was counted as correct if its relative transform matched ground truth within 0.10~m and $5^{\circ}$. Of the 1051 closures accepted across the exploration episodes, 35.2\% were correct (370 closures; interval 17.9 to 58.6\%), and on average 28.2\% of the revisits produced a closure. The precision is a lower bound, because closures are matched to ground truth with a timing bias. It was also low in multi\_room\_loop and office (24.9\% and 27.2\%), the two worlds with the most revisits, a closer analysis of these false closures is planned.

% \paragraph{Navigation.}
Twelve navigation episodes were run, four seeds in each of three worlds, with up to 20 random goals per episode (178 goals in total, as seven episodes ended early because the robot was judged stuck). Nav2 reported 50.0\% of the goals as reached. When checked against the ground-truth position of the robot within 0.30~m, only 29.2\% were reached (52 of 178 goals; interval 16.0 to 40.0\%), and with the stricter 0.15~m threshold the figure was 9.0\%. Averaged per episode, the ground-truth success rate was 23.4\% (interval 2.8 to 46.7\%). The result depended strongly on the world (Table~\ref{tab:nav_results}): 48.8\% in two\_rooms\_door, 20.7\% in office and 2.5\% in maze\_dead\_ends. Where goals were reached, the travelled path was on average 1.21 times the shortest feasible path. The robot triggered about 148 recovery behaviours per episode and recorded 1.9 contact events per episode on average, where a single continuous contact can count as several events.

The gap between the success rate reported by Nav2 and the ground-truth success rate shows that Nav2 often declared a goal reached while the robot was not at the goal, which points to errors in the pose estimate. The mean AMCL position error was 0.318~m (interval 0.183-0.509~m). This diagnostic covers a single episode per setting on a development world, so it is indicative evidence and not a test result. It does suggest that the low success rates in Table~\ref{tab:nav_results} reflect the localisation set-up more than the planner, and they should not be taken as a measure of the RTAB-Map pipeline.

\subsection{Safety and Obstacle Avoidance Performance}
\label{sec:avoid_results}
Obstacle avoidance was tested in two scenarios with three random seeds each (six valid episodes). In \texttt{avoid\_u\_trap} the robot must reach a goal past a static U-shaped trap. In \texttt{avoid\_dynamic\_sudden} a $0.4 \times 0.4$~m box starts moving at 0.5~m/s from behind a wall while the robot drives to its goal. A trial succeeds only if the goal is reached with no contact.

The static scenario succeeded in 3 of 3 trials and the dynamic scenario in 1 of 3, so 4 of 6 overall (66.7\%, 95\% Wilson interval 30.0-90.3\%). The goal was reached in 5 of the 6 trials, and one contact event occurred in total. The mean minimum clearance to an obstacle was 0.159~m (interval 0.076-0.227~m). It is slightly optimistic, because the wheels protrude about 1.8~cm beyond the footprint used for the measurement. With three trials per scenario the intervals are wide. The static trap was handled, but the robot did not reliably avoid the suddenly moving obstacle.

Contact events per episode were 1.78 in exploration, 1.92 in navigation and 0.17 in avoidance. Contact bursts separated by more than 0.5~s are counted separately, so one jam can count three to seven times. The share of obstacle encounters passed without contact was 98.4\% in exploration, 93.2\% in navigation and 80.0\% in the avoidance scenarios, where each episode had only one to three encounters. Walls are excluded from this measure, so \texttt{maze\_dead\_ends} has no encounters. The RMS curvature of the driven path in navigation was 3.01~m$^{-1}$.

\subsection{System Responsiveness, Efficiency and Computational Cost}
\label{sec:sys_perf}
\paragraph{Responsiveness.}
With the SLAM stack running, the time from a velocity command to 50\% of the commanded velocity was 166~ms (interval 160-172~ms; 95th percentile 206~ms), and the time to stop was 260~ms (interval 250-270~ms; 95th percentile 301~ms), from two valid episodes. Both are below the 0.5~s deadline set in advance. The two episodes without the SLAM stack are invalid under the validity rules, which require RTAB-Map updates, and are excluded. The median time from sending a goal to the first valid plan was about 10~ms in navigation and 30~ms in exploration.

The robot passed over mapped cells 1.95 times on average, and 37.6~m$^2$ of free floor was never swept. The mean ground-truth distance travelled was 44.2~m. \section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This work covered the navigation and trash-detection subsystems of Swachh Bauddhika Yantra. The physical prototype was made to perform manual mode, while the software for the navigation subsystem was run in Gazebo, achieving an average of 61.4\% of the reachable floor, with an average trajectory error of 0.57~m. 52 out of 178 navigation goals along with 4 out of 6 obstacle avoidance trials were successful. When ground-truth pose replaced AMCL, 19 out of 20 goals were reached. For trash detection: YOLOV8n, YOLOv11n-seg and RF-DETR were trained on custom made dataset, with all surpassing 0.92 mAP. When deployed on Jetson Orin Nano in FP16, models achieved a maximum of 79 frames per second, while drawing 12.7 to 14.9 watts

The software for the robot was built and tested in Gazebo environment. The three models, YOLOv11n-seg, RF-DETR and YOLO v8n were trained on the custom-made dataset.

On a known map, 29.2\% of 178 navigation goals (52 goals) were reached within 0.30~m of the true position, and obstacle avoidance succeeded in 4 of 6 trials. A diagnostic run, in which 19 of 20 goals were reached with ground-truth localisation against 6 of 19 with AMCL. For trash detection, YOLOv8n, YOLOv11n-seg and RF-DETR Medium were trained on a custom-made dataset of 877 images in 8 classes and reached mAP@0.5 of 0.921, 0.925 and 0.932, respectively. On the Jetson Orin Nano at half precision, they ran at 76.4, 79.3 and 41.4~FPS with a board power of 12.7 to 14.9~W, all above the 30~FPS real-time threshold.

Future work will deploy and validate the navigation software on the physical robot and integrate the navigation and detection subsystems into a single system. It will also address the maze episodes in which no goals were issued and the avoidance of suddenly moving obstacles, and complete the Semi-Automatic and Automatic Modes.

Future work would involve hardware deployment and validation of the software tested on Gazebo. Along with integration of the subsystems into a single unified system. Additionally addressing failures regarding maze episodes without goals and avoidance of moving obstacles, while also finishing Semi-Automatic and Automatic Modes on the physical robot.
